# Critical Discussion, Methods, and Results Draft

## Critical discussion

This project adds four clinically relevant elements to prior work on UCI Heart Disease splits: external site testing, explicit probability calibration, a domain adaptation ablation, and post-hoc explainability tied to clinical plausibility. Recent work has used the UCI Heart Disease data for centralized and federated benchmarking with Shapley-value interpretability, reporting a best centralized SVM accuracy of 83.3% and federated SVM accuracy of 73.8% on the full multi-hospital dataset [Rodriguez and Nafea, 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.06183). Other recent work treats UCI Heart Disease as part of a non-IID tabular federated learning benchmark, emphasizing that heterogeneous clinical clients create joint distribution shift [Obeidi, 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19290). The limitation in these comparisons is that performance is usually summarized as model accuracy or aggregate client performance. In contrast, this analysis asks whether a Cleveland-trained model transfers to Hungarian and Swiss cohorts, whether its probabilities are trustworthy, whether simple unsupervised alignment helps, and whether its learned reasoning resembles clinical intuition.

The best cross-site discriminator was XGBoost + Optuna, but its clinical value depends on calibration rather than AUC alone. On Hungarian external testing, XGBoost achieved the highest ROC-AUC (0.876), average precision (0.834), F1 (0.720), sensitivity (0.632), and high specificity (0.931), narrowly exceeding logistic regression in ROC-AUC (0.869), average precision (0.830), and F1 (0.663). On Swiss external testing, XGBoost also led ROC-AUC (0.720), average precision (0.970), and F1 (0.632), compared with logistic regression ROC-AUC 0.654, average precision 0.954, and F1 0.573. The DeLong comparison supports the same pattern: the XGBoost advantage over logistic regression was statistically significant on Swiss (AUC difference 0.065, p = 0.031) but not on Hungarian (AUC difference 0.006, p = 0.659). However, the Cleveland held-out result was different: logistic regression had the highest ROC-AUC (0.949), F1 (0.864), and sensitivity (0.905), while XGBoost reached ROC-AUC 0.935, F1 0.837, and sensitivity 0.857. Therefore, the argument is not that XGBoost is universally better; it is that the optimized nonlinear model is the strongest external ranking model, while logistic regression remains a robust in-domain baseline.

Calibration changes the clinical interpretation of that win. On Hungarian, Platt calibration gave XGBoost the lowest expected calibration error (ECE = 0.080), slightly better than Platt-calibrated logistic regression (ECE = 0.084) and uncalibrated XGBoost (ECE = 0.089). On Swiss, all models were poorly calibrated because the target prevalence was extreme: Swiss had 115 positives and 8 negatives, a 93.5% positive rate. Even there, isotonic XGBoost had the lowest ECE among XGBoost variants (0.394), better than uncalibrated XGBoost (0.452), but still too high for confident probability-based triage. This is the core clinical trustworthiness finding: XGBoost is useful for ranking patients across external sites, but its raw probabilities should not be treated as reliable risk estimates without calibration and site-specific validation.

The domain adaptation ablation shows that distribution shift is real but simple covariance alignment is not a universal fix. Cleveland to Hungarian had large shifts in restecg (p = 3.58e-40), age (p = 1.76e-13), oldpeak (p = 7.97e-12), slope (p = 1.77e-11), and thalach (p = 3.07e-08). Cleveland to Swiss was even more severe, including cholesterol (Cleveland mean 249.7 vs Swiss mean 0.0, p = 9.94e-95), thalach (149.7 vs 121.6, p = 1.17e-15), sex (0.689 vs 0.919, p = 2.47e-06), and a positive rate shift from 45.8% in Cleveland train to 93.5% in Swiss. CORAL alignment did not improve the optimized XGBoost model: Hungarian ROC-AUC changed from 0.876 to 0.875 (delta -0.000), while F1 fell from 0.720 to 0.627 and sensitivity from 0.632 to 0.491. On Swiss, XGBoost ROC-AUC fell from 0.720 to 0.628, F1 from 0.632 to 0.408, and sensitivity from 0.470 to 0.261. This ablation contributes a negative result: external generalization failure is not solved by unsupervised second-order feature alignment, especially when label prevalence and missingness proxies differ across sites.

SHAP explains why the optimized model is plausible but also why it needs caution. The top XGBoost SHAP features were ca (mean absolute SHAP 0.750), cp_x_thal (0.667), cp (0.320), age_x_oldpeak (0.293), thal (0.262), sex (0.241), age (0.198), thalach (0.195), chol (0.156), and oldpeak (0.119). This aligns with clinical intuition because vessel count, chest pain, thalassemia stress-test findings, age, ST depression, exercise capacity, and sex are established markers in coronary artery disease assessment. At the same time, the importance of engineered interactions such as cp_x_thal and age_x_oldpeak shows that the model relies on combinations of clinical signals rather than single-variable rules. The low SHAP contribution of framingham_proxy (0.032) is also informative: this dataset's predictive signal is driven more by exercise-test and angiographic-proxy variables than by a generic risk-score proxy.

The main limitations are structural. First, the sample size is small: Cleveland train has 212 rows, validation 45, and held-out test 46; Hungarian has 294 rows; Swiss has 123 rows but only 8 negatives. These small denominators make threshold metrics such as F1, sensitivity, specificity, and ECE unstable. Second, there is no temporal validation; the project tests geographic/site transfer but cannot evaluate whether the model remains valid across calendar time. Third, the data are old: the UCI repository records the Heart Disease dataset as donated on June 30, 1988, and the introductory paper was published in 1989 [UCI Heart Disease](https://archive.ics.uci.edu/dataset/45/heart%2Bdisease). Fourth, the processed files do not include explicit patient identifiers, so the Cleveland partitions are checked for row-index overlap but cannot prove patient-level independence beyond the available row records. Finally, the Swiss cohort has severe prevalence shift and unusual cholesterol coding, so its high average precision values should be interpreted alongside the 93.5% positive rate rather than as evidence of deployable performance.

## Methods

The study used the processed Cleveland, Hungarian, and Switzerland files from the UCI Heart Disease repository. The prediction target was binarized as any disease presence versus no disease. Cleveland was split with StratifiedShuffleSplit into 70% train, 15% validation, and 15% held-out test using random state 42. This produced 212 training rows (97 positive, 115 negative; positive rate 45.8%), 45 validation rows (21 positive, 24 negative; positive rate 46.7%), and 46 held-out test rows (21 positive, 25 negative; positive rate 45.7%). Hungarian and Swiss were reserved as external cohorts: Hungarian had 294 rows (106 positive, 188 negative; positive rate 36.1%) and Swiss had 123 rows (115 positive, 8 negative; positive rate 93.5%). Cleveland row-index overlap between train, validation, and test was checked and absent.

All models used the same 13 raw UCI variables: age, sex, cp, trestbps, chol, fbs, restecg, thalach, exang, oldpeak, slope, ca, and thal. The preprocessing pipeline added six engineered features: age_x_thalach, age_x_oldpeak, trestbps_x_chol, cp_x_thal, exang_x_oldpeak, and framingham_proxy. Missing values were imputed with the median and features were standardized.

Three models were optimized using Cleveland train only: logistic regression, XGBoost, and a small multilayer perceptron. Logistic regression used grid search over penalty, solver, class weighting, and regularization strength; the selected model used L2 regularization, C = 0.5, lbfgs, and no class weighting. XGBoost used 50 Optuna trials with the selected parameters n_estimators = 130, max_depth = 5, learning_rate = 0.055, subsample = 0.656, colsample_bytree = 0.666, min_child_weight = 2.752, gamma = 1.812, reg_alpha = 0.051, reg_lambda = 0.506, and scale_pos_weight = 1.029. The MLP used randomized search; the selected model used one hidden layer of 32 units, ReLU activation, alpha = 0.001, batch size 16, and learning_rate_init = 0.0005. Hyperparameter selection used 5-fold stratified cross-validation on Cleveland train, then validation AUC was used as a model-selection sanity check. Final models were refit on Cleveland train plus validation, with Cleveland test, Hungarian, and Swiss untouched until Phase 4 evaluation.

Performance was evaluated with ROC-AUC, average precision, F1, sensitivity, and specificity at a 0.5 threshold. DeLong tests compared the two highest-AUC models within each split. Calibration was evaluated with expected calibration error using 10 uniform probability bins. Platt sigmoid and isotonic calibration were fit using Cleveland validation after training base models on Cleveland train. Domain adaptation was evaluated as an ablation only: CORAL covariance alignment was fit between source train-plus-validation features and each target split, then target features were transformed into the source covariance space before prediction. Explainability used SHAP TreeExplainer on the optimized XGBoost model using up to 150 Cleveland train-plus-validation samples.

## Results

In cross-validation on Cleveland train, the small MLP had the highest mean ROC-AUC (0.912), followed by logistic regression (0.905) and XGBoost + Optuna (0.901). XGBoost had the best mean F1 (0.814) and sensitivity (0.795), while the small MLP had the best specificity (0.930). On Cleveland validation, logistic regression had the highest ROC-AUC (0.903) and average precision (0.918), while XGBoost had the highest F1 (0.789) and specificity (0.917). The selected best model by validation ROC-AUC was logistic regression.

On the untouched Cleveland held-out test, logistic regression performed best: ROC-AUC 0.949, average precision 0.926, F1 0.864, sensitivity 0.905, and specificity 0.840. XGBoost had ROC-AUC 0.935, average precision 0.915, F1 0.837, sensitivity 0.857, and specificity 0.840. The DeLong AUC difference between logistic regression and XGBoost was 0.013 with p = 0.405, so the apparent Cleveland advantage was not statistically significant.

On Hungarian external testing, XGBoost + Optuna was the top model: ROC-AUC 0.876, average precision 0.834, F1 0.720, sensitivity 0.632, and specificity 0.931. Logistic regression was close in discrimination but had lower F1 and sensitivity: ROC-AUC 0.869, average precision 0.830, F1 0.663, sensitivity 0.547, and specificity 0.941. The DeLong AUC difference was 0.006 with p = 0.659.

On Swiss external testing, XGBoost + Optuna again led: ROC-AUC 0.720, average precision 0.970, F1 0.632, sensitivity 0.470, and specificity 0.750. Logistic regression had ROC-AUC 0.654, average precision 0.954, F1 0.573, sensitivity 0.409, and specificity 0.750. The DeLong AUC difference was 0.065 with p = 0.031. The high average precision values should be interpreted cautiously because Swiss prevalence was 93.5% positive.

Calibration was best on Hungarian after Platt calibration for XGBoost: ECE improved from 0.089 uncalibrated to 0.080 with Platt calibration. On Cleveland held-out testing, isotonic logistic regression had the lowest ECE (0.072), while uncalibrated logistic regression had ECE 0.106 and uncalibrated XGBoost had ECE 0.117. On Swiss, calibration remained poor across models, but XGBoost improved from ECE 0.452 uncalibrated to 0.394 with isotonic calibration. These results support calibrated use of probabilities and caution against treating raw model scores as clinical risk estimates.

CORAL domain adaptation produced mixed and mostly negative results. For XGBoost, Hungarian ROC-AUC was essentially unchanged (0.876 without CORAL vs 0.875 with CORAL), but F1 decreased by 0.094 and sensitivity decreased by 0.142. On Swiss, XGBoost ROC-AUC decreased by 0.091, F1 by 0.223, and sensitivity by 0.209. For small MLP, CORAL improved F1 on Swiss from 0.203 to 0.628 and sensitivity from 0.113 to 0.470, but specificity dropped from 1.000 to 0.625 and ROC-AUC improved only from 0.607 to 0.629. The adaptation result is therefore not a deployable improvement; it is evidence that simple unsupervised alignment can shift thresholds and sensitivity/specificity tradeoffs without reliably improving discrimination.
